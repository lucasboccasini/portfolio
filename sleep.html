Recovery starts
with sleep.

Sleep is part of your training routine. A consistent wind-down and enough time to rest can support your energy for the next day.

☾
Home / Sleep

Good recovery is not a reward you earn after a perfect workout. It belongs in the plan. Start with a sleep schedule you can repeat, then adjust it around classes, work, family, and training.

How much sleep should I aim for?

Sleep needs vary with age. The CDC lists these daily ranges for healthy populations:

AGES 6–129–12 hours
AGES 13–178–10 hours
AGES 18–607 or more hours
AGES 61–647–9 hours
AGES 65+7–8 hours

These are general recommendations, not a personal diagnosis. If you often wake unrefreshed or have ongoing trouble sleeping, talk with a healthcare professional.

A 30–60 minute wind-down

Use the last part of the evening to make tomorrow easier and help your day slow down. You do not need an elaborate routine.

  1. Choose a bedtime and wake time you can keep most days.
  2. Set out running clothes, a water bottle, or anything else you need in the morning.
  3. Dim lights and step away from stimulating tasks if that helps you relax.
  4. Try a quiet activity such as reading, gentle stretching, or a warm shower.
  5. Keep the room dark, quiet, and comfortable for you.
Try this tonight

Pick one small cue that tells you the day is ending, like putting your phone on its charger and setting out tomorrow’s clothes.

Sleep and your running routine

When your schedule is busy, avoid treating sleep as the thing that always gets cut first. If a planned run competes with needed rest, consider shifting the run, making it easier, or taking a rest day. A routine that adapts is easier to sustain.

Quick reset

Make your evening routine easy to repeat.

  • Set a steady sleep window.
  • Prepare for the morning.
  • Make the room comfortable.
  • Ask for help if sleep problems persist.
